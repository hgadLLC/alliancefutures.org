The Great Geo-Patriation: The New Test of Sovereignty

By Andrew Horton · Senior Fellow · October 12, 2026
Download PDF

On 24 October, Amazon Web Services will deliberately break one of the central assumptions of the digital age. For several hours, the world’s largest cloud provider will disconnect its European Sovereign Cloud from the global network on which it was built. The experiment is ostensibly technical. Its implications are geopolitical. It asks a question every government will eventually have to answer: what still works when the connection disappears?

For three decades, the global economy was built on the assumption that digital dependencies would expand indefinitely. That architecture of integration is now colliding with an era of great power rivalry and cyber warfare. The systems that sustain modern societies—from power grids and financial markets to frontier artificial intelligence—run on infrastructure operating largely beyond national control. Connectivity defined the last strategic era. Operational sovereignty will define the next.

Geography tells you where a system sits. Sovereignty tells you who remains in command when the links fail.

For years, governments and corporations assumed sovereignty was a matter of geography: store data onshore, in domestic facilities in Sydney, Ottawa or Osaka, and sovereign control would follow. Gartner calls the resulting retreat from global platforms geo-patriation. The evidence increasingly suggests a server bolted to sovereign soil remains strategically hostage if it depends on foreign identity services, offshore encryption authorities and external software updates to function. Geography tells you where a system sits. Sovereignty tells you who remains in command when the links fail.

A new doctrine is emerging at the centre of digital statecraft. Severability is the capacity of a nation’s critical systems to continue functioning under domestic control after its foreign connections have been interrupted. Untested sovereignty is a theory awaiting its first crisis.

A nation controls a system only to the extent that it can sever it, and a network that has never been severed has never been proven sovereign. Allies on three continents are now acting on that logic, and their spur has come from dependence on friends as much as fear of foes. In March, Japan’s Digital Agency admitted a domestic provider to its government cloud for the first time, breaking the exclusive hold of American hyperscalers on grounds of economic security. In May, Ottawa, Washington’s closest neighbour, advanced a large sovereign AI data centre with TELUS. When Tokyo and Ottawa hedge against American platforms, the shift is structural.

Europe arrived at the conclusion first, and its catalyst was a revelation of allied reach. On 10 June 2025, a Microsoft France executive testified under oath to a French Senate inquiry that he could not guarantee French citizens’ data would be withheld from United States authorities without French consent. A tedious legal debate became a crisis of strategic autonomy almost overnight. The European Commission’s proposed Cloud and AI Development Act, published on 3 June 2026, would grade cloud services across four assurance levels, culminating in full, verifiable control over the software supply chain.

Artificial intelligence is weaponising this dependency. As governments integrate AI into the machinery of the state, they are moving beyond data storage to the automation of judgement. Frontier models are increasingly being used to analyse intelligence, assess risks and shape policy. A government that performs these functions inside an environment it does not ultimately control has not merely outsourced infrastructure. It has outsourced a portion of its strategic autonomy.

The threat matrix is unforgiving. In November 2025, Anthropic disclosed that a Chinese state-sponsored group had turned Claude Code against roughly thirty organisations, with AI carrying out as much as 90 per cent of the tactical work. The significance was not the scale of the campaign but the automation of the attacker. Credential discovery, vulnerability analysis and supply-chain mapping, once the preserve of highly trained operators, now execute at machine speed. American cyber agencies have warned that Chinese state actors are pre-positioning inside critical infrastructure networks for disruptive or destructive attacks in a future crisis or conflict. An adversary preparing for war wants leverage over the nervous system of the target state, and the sovereignty debate must graduate from the civilian concern of data privacy to the strategic reality of dependency management.

The most instructive blueprint for digital resilience comes from the physical world. For decades, Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania generated electricity on sovereign territory while strategic control of their grid remained in Moscow. On 8 February 2025 the Baltic operators took a historic gamble and cut the cord, running their grids entirely alone in island mode before synchronising with continental Europe the next day. They demonstrated the capacity to stand alone before choosing with whom to connect, and that sequence is the essence of modern statecraft. Permanent dependence creates vulnerability. Permanent isolation creates weakness. Sovereignty is the hard-won space between the two.

Critics will rightly point out that permanently severed digital systems degrade. Threat intelligence, security patches and algorithmic improvements flow through global networks, and a static system inevitably becomes an insecure one. The objection clarifies the doctrine. Severability means strategic choice: the freedom to absorb a catastrophic shock, keep the lights on, reroute dependencies and reconnect on favourable terms. The Baltic states stood alone briefly. What mattered was that they could, and permanent self-sufficiency is a different and weaker ambition.

Australia has begun to apply this doctrine at the apex of its national security apparatus, commissioning a Top Secret cloud operated by AWS under a contract worth at least $2 billion over a decade. The more consequential vulnerability sits lower in the stack, across the civilian platforms on which governments govern and economies function. The Digital Transformation Agency’s Hosting Certification Framework tests providers on ownership and control and stops short of the ultimate test.

Canberra should add a severability tier to that framework, and allied democracies should mandate the same for their critical infrastructure. Any provider of essential government services, explicitly including frontier AI platforms, must prove through independent audit that its systems operate securely when offshore control links are physically removed. In Australia, the standard belongs in the Security of Critical Infrastructure regime, mutually recognised across the Five Eyes, Europe, Japan and Canada. Democracies hold the collective procurement power to force this market shift. Authoritarian regimes, whose systems are inextricably bound to the party-state, can never offer genuine, auditable severability.

Corporations must embrace the same discipline. Directors should require their providers to run a 72-hour dark test, operating critical workloads without access to a global control plane, to see what breaks. Many boards believe they are managing digital infrastructure; in truth, they are managing untested assumptions. A board that buys cloud on price alone is carrying a sovereign risk it has never measured, and the first honest measurement may come from an adversary.

In 1902, engineers completed the Pacific Cable to link Australia to Britain through territory the Empire controlled. They understood that communications passing through someone else’s switchboard could ultimately be controlled by someone else’s government. The technologies have changed; the strategic logic has not. The era of digital globalisation rewarded connection. The era now emerging will reward survivability. For thirty years, governments measured digital power by reach, scale and integration. The next thirty years will measure it by something far simpler: the ability to absorb disruption without surrendering control. Sovereignty is not the ability to connect. It is the ability to keep functioning when connection is denied. In the age of AI, the states that prevail will not necessarily be those with the biggest models or the largest data centres. They will be the states that preserve agency under pressure, maintain essential functions through shock and retain the freedom to choose their own course. Ultimately, every claim of digital sovereignty will face the same test. What still worked when the line was cut?
