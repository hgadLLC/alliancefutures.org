For a decade, the settled view in Western defence and intelligence circles held that demography was the one constraint the Chinese Communist Party could neither engineer nor legislate away. A shrinking workforce, the argument ran, would drive up labour costs, drain regional pension funds and place a hard ceiling on the growth from which the party derives its legitimacy. The thesis carried a hidden vulnerability: it rested on the industrial-age assumption that a nation&rsquo;s labour supply is a biological endowment, bounded by the births of a generation earlier. That assumption has expired.

What is taking shape across China&rsquo;s industrial heartlands is a policy of **manufactured demography**: the systematic conversion of labour supply from an organic demographic variable into a planned, scalable and exportable industrial output. If Beijing succeeds, it will do more than survive its demographic crisis. It will export its solution, and with it structural authority over the physical engine of the twenty-first-century economy.

Western capitals are transfixed by the machines. The contest will be decided by the rules the machines obey. In February, Beijing published the world&rsquo;s first full-lifecycle national standard system for humanoid robots and embodied artificial intelligence, drafted in nine weeks by more than 120 state institutions, research laboratories and private champions including Unitree and AgiBot, and spanning six pillars from intelligent computing to physical safety and operational ethics. The 15th Five-Year Plan simultaneously elevated embodied intelligence to a priority future industry and embedded component localisation targets directly in top-tier planning documents. The sequence is deliberate and historically familiar. Standards precede scale, and scale commands markets.

Scale is arriving quickly. Global shipments of embodied-AI industrial units reached 18,000 in 2025 and are on track to exceed 50,000 this year, with Chinese firms supplying more than 45 per cent of the market. Last year more than 140 Chinese manufacturers unveiled over 330 distinct humanoid models. Unitree priced its R1 humanoid at **US$5,900**, and manufacturing costs are falling 40 per cent year on year &mdash; double the pace Western analysts projected. Machine labour is descending the cost curve that solar panels, batteries and electric vehicles descended before it, toward the same destination: Chinese dominance of a category the West invented.

The strategic mechanics mirror the mid-twentieth-century revolution in logistics. Malcom McLean&rsquo;s steel shipping container was rudimentary engineering. The economic revolution arrived in 1968, when the International Organization for Standardization locked in its dimensions and corner fittings, and every crane, ship, railcar and loading dock on earth adjusted to a single specification. The ports that standardised early &mdash; Singapore, Rotterdam &mdash; became the choke points of global trade, while London&rsquo;s docks, which resisted, were derelict within a decade. The invention created the possibility. The standard captured the century&rsquo;s rents.

<blockquote class="pullquote">Manufactured demography converts labour supply from an organic demographic variable into a planned, scalable and exportable industrial output. The state that standardises machine labour first will dictate the terms on which every other nation puts machines to work.</blockquote>

Beijing understands this history. A humanoid robot on a production line in Southeast Asia, Latin America or Europe is a vector of strategic influence as much as a piece of capital equipment: every unit carries safety certifications, data-routing protocols, remote diagnostics and firmware dependencies defined in Beijing. As Chinese humanoids populate assembly lines from Jakarta to Lagos, the operating norms of global production will compile in Mandarin. A workforce that is manufactured rather than born can also be **surged at will** &mdash; operating continuously in high-threat environments, staffing defence industrial bases in crisis without political backlash, and executing logistics, sustainment and grey-zone tasks long before a shot is fired.

For the democracies, the stakes extend well beyond trade. The mass export of subsidised, state-standardised machine labour presents three compounding dangers. Enterprises will face a choice between importing cheap and efficient Chinese synthetic labour or conceding competitiveness, and acceptance risks hollowing out industrial wages and deepening social fracture. Connecting Chinese embodied systems to Western infrastructure, energy grids and factories creates espionage, telemetry and kill-switch vulnerabilities of a kind no democracy has yet priced. And if Beijing comes to dominate the International Organization for Standardization and the International Electrotechnical Commission, democratic nations will find themselves adopting technical rules drafted by an authoritarian state, eroding their own benchmarks for labour safety, data custody and human oversight.

The window to contest this trajectory is short but open, because Beijing&rsquo;s position carries honest weaknesses. UBTech concedes its current models reach **30 to 50 per cent of human productivity** in narrow, controlled tasks, with runtimes of a few hours per charge, and most of last year&rsquo;s deployments were pilots rather than production. More telling, China still depends on the democracies for its highest-precision hardware &mdash; the harmonic reducers, ball screws and high-torque servo motors supplied by Japanese and German engineering firms. The component choke points and the credibility of the standards institutions both remain in democratic hands.

The counter-strategy writes itself, and it demands precisely the horizon-thinking that crisis management crowds out. Treat precision actuators, sensors and gearheads with the stringency applied to advanced lithography, and deny Beijing the components on which reliable scale depends. Contest every seat and working group in the international standards bodies, because Beijing is already tabling pre-drafted specifications and unified democratic alternatives must be ready to meet them. And build an allied certification regime for trusted machine labour &mdash; Washington, Tokyo, Berlin, London, Seoul and Canberra each hold a piece of it &mdash; setting joint benchmarks for data security, hardware provenance and human-in-the-loop oversight, so that allied markets form a protected sphere that untrusted, state-subsidised systems cannot enter.

<p class="signoff">Demography is no longer destiny; it is an arena of great-power competition. China&rsquo;s planners have concluded that the workforce of the twenty-first century will be designed, mass-produced, certified and exported, and they intend to write its specifications. The democracies still hold the components, the research ecosystems and the institutional trust to lead the transformation instead &mdash; and the leaders who grasp this now will be planning for the labour market of 2035 while their rivals manage the politics of the quarter. The census measured the power of nations in the last century. The machine standard will measure it in the next.</p>
